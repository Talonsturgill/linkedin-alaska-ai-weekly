The Air Force is about to spend roughly $400 million on AI workloads anchored at JBER and Eielson over the next 18 months. Almost no Alaska firms are on the contract sheet.

Most of the dollar value is moving through three vehicles the AK industrial base hasn’t trained for. Pallet contracts under DAF AFLCMC, the Indefinite Delivery Indefinite Quantity awards the 354th and 673rd wings issue against their Operational Imperative line, and the Tradewind OTA pool that the Chief Digital and AI Office runs. AK firms that have built solid commercial books in fisheries data, mining ops, or rural telehealth are the right technical match for the workloads. They’re the wrong administrative match for the paperwork.

The conventional framing is that this is a workforce problem. More CS graduates, more bootcamps, more federal STEM dollars, and AK closes the gap. That framing is structurally wrong. The gap isn’t engineers. The gap is the SAM.gov registration, the DCAA-acceptable accounting system, the CMMC Level 2 certification, and the past-performance file program managers actually read at source selection. Those are administrative artifacts, not engineering ones, and AK firms can buy them in months if anyone gives them a clean roadmap.

The third path nobody is naming is a coordinated AK industrial onramp run by the State of Alaska, the regional ANCs, and a single anchor prime. Pick the workloads first. Pair them to AK domain expertise. Then handle the compliance bundle as a shared service so individual firms aren’t each rebuilding the same back office.

Three workload categories are the obvious starting points.

- Maritime domain awareness, where Coast Guard District 17 is piloting AI-assisted vessel tracking and AK fisheries data shops have the labeling expertise contractors in the Lower 48 don’t.
- Arctic logistics and predictive maintenance, where the 354th Maintenance Group at Eielson is moving toward AI condition-based maintenance on F-35 sustainment and AK aviation operators have decades of cold-weather telemetry.
- Tribal and rural telehealth inference, where IHS and the Alaska Native Tribal Health Consortium have built the only at-scale rural AI healthcare deployments in the country, and CDAO is actively procuring lessons learned.

The stakes are about lock-in. Federal procurement cycles run on 5-year IDIQs. Whoever wins the first wave of these awards in fiscal ’26 and ’27 will hold the past-performance advantage when the same workloads recompete in ’30 and ’32. If outside primes lock those positions now, AK firms become subcontractors on their own home turf. That isn’t a workforce outcome anyone in Juneau says they want, and it isn’t a sovereignty outcome the regional ANCs say they want.

There’s a less obvious risk too. CDAO is openly looking to procure differently. They want fewer large primes, more performers, and faster contract vehicle entry. Tradewind has issued more than $700 million in awards in 24 months, and most went to firms with under 200 employees. Alaska is the kind of place that vehicle was designed for. If the state’s industrial base shows up on the SAM.gov rolls in the next 12 months, AK fits the shape of where the money is moving. If it doesn’t, the door closes for another procurement cycle and the workloads ship to firms in Northern Virginia who don’t understand the operating environment.

What’s the right convener to run that AK industrial onramp, the State, the ANCs, an anchor prime, or a delegation-led consortium?
