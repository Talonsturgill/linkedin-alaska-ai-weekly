The FCC’s second-phase submarine cable landing rules took effect July 8, 2026 (DA-26-684). They decide who can close the GCI Liberty and Quintillion transfers that carry Alaska’s only Arctic fiber.

A cable-landing-license transfer is not a single FCC approval. Before any change of ownership can be granted, the filing has to clear an FCC rule regime and a separate executive-branch national-security review that can freeze it or force mitigation.

The layers, from the docket.

- Statutory delegation. The Submarine Cable Landing License Act of 1921 hands the President’s licensing power to the FCC, the gatekeeper for every landing station and transfer of control.
- FCC intake and second-phase rules. OI Docket 24-523 governs how transfer filings are judged. The phase-two rules effective July 8 add foreign-carrier-affiliation approval, a security certification, and capacity reporting. Held by the FCC Office of International Affairs.
- Foreign-adversary presumption. Report and Order FCC 25-49 presumptively bars a license for any entity owned or directed by a foreign adversary, set by the full Commission.
- Executive-branch national-security review. The FCC refers each application to the Department of State and an interagency committee that can object or demand binding mitigation. Held by the Committee for the Assessment of Foreign Participation, chaired by the Attorney General.

The leverage does not sit at the FCC. It sits in that fourth layer. That committee, chaired by the Attorney General with Defense and Homeland Security, owns one binary on each referred transfer. File an objection, or return no objection and let the FCC grant. Quintillion’s modification (SCL-MOD-20250930-00070) went to State on Feb 17, 2026, the GCI Liberty transfer applications on Mar 20, 2026. The FCC will not grant over an unresolved objection, so one filing from that single body freezes both deals.

This is a case where the visible regulator is not the gate. The July 8 rules tighten the standard, but the real reprice-or-block authority is parked in a DOJ-chaired committee that works by referral and never shows up on the FCC docket surface. Quintillion is Alaska’s only U.S.-Arctic subsea fiber operator, the connectivity substrate under any North Slope or Railbelt AI data center. The whole Arctic-fiber thesis runs through one national-security veto whose timing no licensee controls.

If you are underwriting an Alaska AI-data-center site tied to Arctic fiber, treat Team Telecom clearance, not the FCC’s ministerial grant, as the binding closing condition. Stress-test the schedule and financing against a mitigation-agreement scenario, not an unconditional early-2027 in-service date.

If the Committee returns an objection instead of clearance on the Quintillion referral, who carries the repriced risk, the buyer, the lenders, or the data-center offtaker counting on early-2027 fiber?
