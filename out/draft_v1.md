This week autonomous wildfire drones started flying real suppression runs over a 1,000 square kilometer zone in Alaska’s NANA region, with UAF’s ACUASI as test partner. The same seven days, Washington and Juneau moved to cut or stall the inputs those systems run on.

Start with the data. On June 15 Sen. Lisa Murkowski co-led a bipartisan ten-senator letter to NSF urging the agency to halt dismantlement of the Ocean Observatories Initiative, a $386M network of more than 900 deep-sea sensors with moorings in the Gulf of Alaska. NSF calls the removal a budget descoping. The ocean condition data those instruments collect feeds the models NOAA and UAF use to set catch limits in a roughly $5.3B fishery, and increasingly it’s the kind of long-horizon time series AI stock assessment is built on. You can’t run fisheries models on data you’ve stopped collecting, and once the moorings come out the time series breaks. NSF’s timeline runs straight into the December 2026 harvest specification cycle.

Then the power. On June 12 the Alaska House passed an 80-to-90 percent property-tax cut for the Alaska LNG pipeline 34-5, a break developer Glenfarne says it needs to reach a final investment decision. As of June 16, according to Alaska Public Media, senators were still weighing project risk with the special session’s June 21 deadline approaching and no clear Senate floor timeline. That in-state gas is the same supply Alaska’s cheap-power compute pitch depends on. The Air Force’s AI-data-center solicitation at JBER, Eielson, and Clear SFS has proposals due June 29, and Golden Valley Electric’s fuel charge just hit a record 20.7 cents per kWh. Untenable math for a 100-plus megawatt load.

The one input that advanced was connectivity. GCI announced June 11 it’s adding Starlink bonded gateways at Bethel, Sitka, Kotzebue, and Dillingham, a high-capacity middle-mile failover tier that gives those hubs a credible backhaul argument for edge inference. Telecompetitor reports the model is already live at Unalaska and Nome.

So the substrate isn’t retreating uniformly, but two of the three inputs wobbled in one week.

The pattern worth naming for anyone underwriting an Alaska AI project is the gap between demand and feedstock. Drones land on Alaska soil while the budget and the Legislature underfund the data and the power those systems consume. Data is the cheapest input to lose and the hardest to rebuild.

Which input layer should Alaska defend first, the ocean data, the gas, or the bandwidth?
