Stak Energy's proposed North Slope AI data center cleared a preliminary Alaska DNR land lease finding this week. The permitting stack it now faces is longer than any press release acknowledges.

The mechanism is a sequential state-and-federal authorization chain that any developer must clear to build a large-scale AI campus on Alaska state land near the Dalton Highway, running from a DNR best-interest finding through federal Clean Water Act permitting before a single cubic yard of gravel moves.

The layers, in order:

- Alaska DNR Best-Interest Finding and 50-Year Land Lease — DNR Division of Oil and Gas issues a preliminary finding under AS 38.05.070(d); no final finding means no lease and no construction start. Controlling actor: Alaska DNR, Division of Oil and Gas (Commissioner-delegated authority)
- Public Comment and Administrative Appeal Window — any party submitting written comments by June 15, 2026 earns standing to appeal the Final Best-Interest Finding under AS 38.05.035(i)-(m) to Alaska Superior Court; a successful appeal halts the lease indefinitely. Controlling actor: public commenters (standing gate) and Alaska Superior Court (appellate authority)
- U.S. Army Corps of Engineers CWA Section 404 Authorization — Stak Energy must obtain federal authorization before discharging roughly 7 million cubic yards of gravel fill to elevate power plants and compute off the tundra. No application filed as of May 2026. Controlling actor: Army Corps Alaska District (POA), District Engineer under 33 U.S.C. § 1344

The chokepoint is Layer 3. The Army Corps Alaska District District Engineer owns a binary decision, authorize or deny the Section 404 permit for roughly 7 million cubic yards of gravel fill. No fill authorization means no gravel pad, and no gravel pad means the campus doesn't get built. That 7 million cubic yard figure is nearly double the fill volume authorized for the Willow oil project, and Willow was a resource-extraction project with decades of Arctic NEPA precedent behind it. Stak Energy has no application filed, no NEPA scope set, and no comparable precedent for a non-extraction project at this scale in Arctic Alaska.

The structural read isn't complicated. The DNR preliminary finding is a real step forward, and the summer 2026 construction timeline in the Alaska Public Media report is structurally impossible. The Army Corps takes 12 to 24 months on standard Section 404 permits. A permit of this fill volume and environmental complexity, with no application filed as of May 30, 2026, will not resolve in time for a summer 2026 shovel. What exists today is a land lease pathway and a press narrative. The federal permitting machine that actually controls the clock hasn't started yet.

Alaska AI infrastructure investors and operators should treat the Army Corps Alaska District public notice register, not the DNR lease date and not Stak's stated timeline, as the real project clock. Nothing appears on that register as of today. When an application is filed and a NEPA determination is initiated, that's the signal worth tracking. Until then, the project is a planning document with a construction date attached.
