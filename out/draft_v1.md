Koniag’s CEO told Alaska Business Magazine that one of its AI solutions “saves the Army more than $37 million annually.” The capability is real. The accounting word is off, and the difference is the part that matters to an auditor.

That figure comes from Ron Unger, Chairman and CEO of Koniag, in the December 1 2025 Regional Roundup. His point was that an Alaska Native Corporation can build and deliver AI to a federal customer, not just pass work through. On that, he’s right. Koniag Government Services built the DORA bot, an automated vendor-eligibility tool now running at scale across Army procurement. A published AWS case study carries the same $37 million number. For a reader sizing up ANC capability in the federal AI lane, this reads like hard proof.

It mostly is. The DORA bot works. The AWS case study credits it with 687,000 labor-hours saved a year. Koniag’s own VP of AI Strategy, Carin Chapman, frames it cleanly. “It doesn’t replace the professional. It gives them back nearly an hour each time.” That’s a genuine, defensible result, and the kind of domain-built automation Alaska firms should be winning.

Here’s the one load-bearing word. The AWS case study calls the $37 million “annual cost avoidance,” not savings. Those are different categories, and GAO treats them as different. Cost avoidance means hours not spent. The Army’s appropriation doesn’t shrink. Savings means actual outlays come down. When “cost avoidance” gets repeated as “saves,” the claim quietly migrates from a labor-efficiency story (true) to a budget-impact story (not what the source says).

Why that distinction earns its keep with this audience.

- Cost avoidance frees staff time. It does not return dollars to the appropriation.
- Auditors and appropriators score the two on separate ledgers.
- A contracting officer can’t book avoided hours as a budget reduction.
- ROI math built on “savings” inflates the perceived return of the work.

The stakes sit in the ANC 8(a) lane Koniag competes in. That lane has no revenue cap and allows sole-source awards above the small-business ceiling. A “$37 million in savings” framing taken at face value overstates the auditable ROI of ANC-prime AI work. It can pull more sole-source task orders on a premise of realized budget impact that the source never claimed. And it parks the recompete risk on the ANC, because when auditors apply the cost-avoidance standard and find no realized savings, the gap lands on Koniag’s past-performance file, not the customer’s.

The capability is the asset here. The number is real. Does the federal AI market reward ANCs that report avoided hours as precisely as it rewards those who claim dollars saved?
