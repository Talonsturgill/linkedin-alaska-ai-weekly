Governor Dunleavy keeps pitching Alaska as a data hub for three continents. The geography is real. The cable that would carry that traffic does not yet exist.

At a data-center investment event reported by Broadband Breakfast on April 24, 2026, the Governor said Alaska would “embark upon a very robust fiber process so Alaska can become the center of data transmission for three continents.” He has run the same three-continent framing at Data Center World last October and at CES this January, so this is settled recruitment doctrine, not an offhand line.

The pitch is grounded. Alaska's Arctic position really is among the shortest routing paths between Asia, Europe, and North America, and proposed subsea projects have flagged Alaskan routes as the short way across. Capital is moving too. GCI's $310 million acquisition of Quintillion builds a self-healing ring of roughly 3,316 route miles, and cold-climate sites post strong power-usage-effectiveness. Anyone who has read those facts can fairly conclude the hub thesis has bones.

Here is where the framing breaks. No intercontinental cable linking Alaska to Asia or Europe is contracted, financed, or under construction. The GCI-Quintillion ring announced April 22, 2026 is entirely intra-Alaska, with no intercontinental leg at all. And the one Arctic subsea cable that does exist, Quintillion's, was severed by Beaufort Sea ice scour in January 2025, knocking roughly 20,000 Northwest Alaska residents offline until late summer. Per the Anchorage Daily News, that was the second break at nearly the same spot in two years. The first came in June 2023. Arctic subsea reliability is not a solved problem. It is an open engineering question with a two-for-two failure record.

The more accurate frame is this. Alaska has genuine geographic and infrastructure assets that make intercontinental routing plausible, but the three-continent hub is an aspirational 2031-plus scenario, not a near-term fact. Allocators should price it that way.

That distinction has a balance-sheet cost. Data-center operators, cloud providers, and infrastructure investors are being recruited on the hub premise. Site selection, long-lead procurement, permitting, and multi-year power contracts written against an uncontracted cable carry stranded-investment risk. AIDEA and DCCED are marketing the opportunity, and developers are sizing timelines off the same pitch. When the binding asset, an intercontinental cable with proven ice survivability, is the one piece missing, optimistic sequencing becomes capital exposure.

If you were underwriting an Alaska data-center site today, what milestone would you require before you priced the three-continent hub as anything other than aspirational?
