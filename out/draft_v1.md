Governor Mike Dunleavy told a data-center crowd in Austin last October that Alaska’s cold could save a one-gigawatt plant up to $150 million a year on cooling. The PUE math is real. The site-selection conclusion isn’t.

Speaking at Data Center World Power and reported by Data Center Knowledge, Dunleavy said Alaska runs about 30 degrees cooler than Texas on average, which on the PUE line could save a one-gigawatt plant upwards of $150 million a year. The cooling pitch has traveled. Far North Digital co-founder and former Anchorage Mayor Ethan Berkowitz made the same case for the North Slope in a January ADN op-ed, and analyst Christopher Tozzi amplified it in Data Center Knowledge in April.

It’s an easy case to believe because the physics holds. Free-air and adiabatic cooling are proven. Nordic hyperscalers in Iceland, Finland, and Sweden run lower PUE for exactly this reason, and Alaska genuinely is roughly 30 degrees cooler than Texas. A governor doing PUE math is using real industry metrics, not inventing them. If cooling were a big share of a one-gigawatt facility’s draw, the savings really would be material.

Here’s where it breaks. Cooling isn’t a big share of the draw. UAF’s Alaska Center for Energy and Power found that cooling costs typically represent 5 percent or less of ongoing operations and maintenance costs, and warned that the cold winters may not offer quite as many thermal advantages as one would think. The same group flagged the line that actually decides the deal. Alaska’s cost of electricity is among the highest in the US.

So the cooling figure is accurate in isolation and misleading as a siting argument. It spotlights the smallest cost lever while the largest one runs the wrong way.

- Cooling is typically 5 percent or less of O&M, per UAF ACEP.
- Delivered power is the dominant operating cost, and Alaska’s runs among the highest in the country.
- Free cooling is a tiebreaker, not a thesis, once the power price is competitive.

For anyone underwriting an Alaska bid, this matters at the model. A developer who anchors on the cooling pitch is optimizing the 5 percent line while the dominant operating cost stays unresolved, and it stays unresolved absent Alaska LNG financial close or a serious renewable build. Alaska can make an honest hyperscale pitch. It just has to lead with the power-price fix, not the thermometer.

If you were modeling a one-gigawatt Alaska data center today, what delivered-power number would have to clear before free cooling is even worth mentioning?
