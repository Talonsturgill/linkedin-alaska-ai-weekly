A Council 30 president told Alaska to fear a federal AI bill that erases state worker protections. The bill now on the table does close to the opposite, and the real Alaska risk sits in Juneau.

Writing in the Anchorage Daily News on June 1, LeiLauni Scheideman, president of the Alaska Airlines Flight Attendants Council 30 (AFA-CWA), warned that “Congress is right now considering federal legislation that would override state laws protecting workers, kids and communities from the harmful impacts of AI.” For a union representing flight attendants at a tech-adjacent employer, that is a serious and reasonable alarm to raise.

It is reasonable for good reasons. Earlier moratorium drafts in this Congress did propose sweeping preemption of all state AI regulation, and the AFL-CIO has campaigned hard against federal preemption as a worker-protection threat. The legal line between regulating how AI is built and how it gets used has never been clean, and critics fairly argue a federal floor can quietly become a federal ceiling. The fight is live and worth having.

Here is what the alarm leaves out. The bill actually on the table is the Obernolte-Trahan bipartisan discussion draft, reported by Roll Call on June 4, three days after the op-ed ran. As drafted, it preempts only state laws “specifically regulating the development” of AI models for three years, and it states plainly that “preemption would not apply to laws related to the use or deployment of AI models.” Use and deployment is exactly where worker, child-safety, and consumer protections live. This draft carves out the very category Scheideman is fighting to protect.

The more accurate frame flips the threat. Alaska has no enacted state AI law for Washington to override. HCR 3, the only Alaska AI task-force measure, has sat in House Finance since its last recorded action on May 16, 2025, with no floor vote. So the exposure is not erasure. It is inertia. A federal development-only moratorium could drain the urgency for the Alaska Legislature to pass any workplace AI rule during the three-year window, leaving workers with nothing on the state books if Congress never fills the gap.

That reframes the ask for Alaska employers and unions at Alaska Airlines and GCI, and for the delegation’s eventual vote. The federal draft is a floor to build on, not a wall to fear. The open question is whether Juneau treats it that way before the window closes.

If the federal bill protects use and deployment, should Alaska be racing to pass its own workplace AI law instead of fighting the preemption?
