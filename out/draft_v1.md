Every standalone AI bill in Alaska died at the May 20 adjournment. HB 217 on autonomous vehicles, SB 2 on state-agency AI and deepfake disclosure, HB 47, and HCR 3, the AI study task force that expired with no recommendations.

The Anchorage Daily News reported May 28 that more than 80% of bills failed this session. The lone AI-adjacent survivor, HB 239's criminal penalty for AI-generated CSAM, sits on Gov. Dunleavy's desk after transmission June 1, with an 18-day clock ending June 18. So Alaska enters the back half of 2026 with no AV rule, no agency AI policy, no deepfake standard, and no task force.

The conventional read is that this is a light-touch win. A regulatory vacuum reads as pro-business until you ask who fills it. The answer is not Juneau, because three things aren't waiting for a statute.

- The money. Alaska's $272.2M Rural Health Transformation grant opened its application portal June 1 and closes June 22, with a tech-innovation tranche of up to roughly $20M funding AI clinical decision support, remote diagnostics, and drone pharmacy delivery. 403 organizations advanced past the Letter of Interest stage.
- The deployment. The Division of Forestry's infrared-drone UAS module reportedly contained the Sawmill Creek Fire near Delta Junction at 14.8 acres over May 20 to 22. Real robotics in state operations, fire season opening, no state framework behind it.
- The capital math. Glenfarne disclosed June 3 to a special session that Alaska LNG Phase 1 will cost $44.5B to $54.5B, above the Department of Revenue's prior $46.2B estimate. Gigawatt-class data-center pitches in this state lean on North Slope gas, so that number quietly resets the power math under every one of them.

Here is the structural problem. The framework Alaska didn't write is being written anyway, in Washington and in federal grant offices. The June 2 White House AI executive order put CISA on a 30-day clock to July 2 to extend AI cyber tools to rural hospitals, community banks, and local utilities. NSF's TechAccess program sets a June 16 letter-of-intent deadline for the state's AI coordination hub. Those are rulebooks, and Alaska's seat at the drafting table is empty.

The lock-in is that grant terms and federal cyber standards become the de facto state policy once Alaska builds on them. By the time the 35th Legislature convenes in January, the operating rules for AI in Alaska health, fire, and energy will already be set, by people who don't answer to Juneau voters.

If the legislature won't write the rulebook, who should, the delegation, the agencies, or the grant offices already doing it by default?
