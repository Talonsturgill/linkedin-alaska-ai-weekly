DOE just awarded $89 million to a coal plant in Alaska to power AI data centers. The real option on that investment isn't in Washington. It's on the desk of one state transportation commissioner in Juneau.

The Terra Energy Center DOE Defense Production Act Coal Grant Stack is the mechanism that matters. A DPA Title III authorization lets DOE channel funds directly to coal construction without a standard competitive grant process, bypassing the review structure that normally gates this kind of spend. That's the federal layer. Below it, the project gets complicated fast.

The anatomy:

- L1 — DOE Defense Production Act Title III Authority — Presidential DPA invocation channels coal construction funds without competitive grant process, Secretary of Energy Chris Wright
- L2 — DOE Grant Award to Terra Energy Center Corp. — $89M federal share ($190.4M total with dollar-for-dollar match) funds feasibility and pre-construction on the proposed 1.25-GW plant near Skwentna, DOE Office of Clean Energy Demonstrations
- L3 — West Susitna Access Road NEPA EIS — a required 22-mile gravel road crossing 180-plus streams must clear a full Environmental Impact Statement before any coal mine can be reached, Alaska DOT&PF Commissioner Ryan Anderson, P.E.
- L4 — Matanuska Electric Association Binding PPA — MEA's board must convert the existing nonbinding term sheet into a binding long-term power purchase agreement before the project reaches financial close, MEA Board of Directors
- L5 — Greenfield Coal Mine Permitting Stack — a mine that doesn't exist yet must obtain ADNR lease, BLM surface permit, MSHA mine plan, and ADEC air and water permits, ADNR Commissioner, BLM Alaska State Director, MSHA, and ADEC Commissioner

Commissioner Anderson owns the chokepoint. His Record of Decision on the West Susitna Access Road EIS is the one gate no federal agency, no grant office, and no MEA board vote can substitute for. The road is the only viable access route to the only viable coal mine site. Sign the ROD and construction can begin, the mine site becomes accessible, and the full $190M-plus investment stack has a path. Withhold or remand it and the plant has no fuel supply, the DOE grant is stranded, and the AI baseload story ends there. Programmatic Agreement materials released March 17, 2026 put the ROD no earlier than late 2027.

The DOE announcement signals political will. It doesn't signal construction-readiness. According to Latitude Media, the project site sits 70 miles from the nearest transmission line, and the mine needed to feed the plant doesn't yet exist, per E&E News. Coal-for-AI in Alaska is a five-year infrastructure buildout that runs through a state environmental review desk, not a federal grant office. The grant agreement execution is expected within 90 days of June 4 and will contain feasibility-study milestones. Those milestones will reveal whether the timeline anyone in Washington is describing is credible, or whether the 2030-plus delivery window makes this irrelevant to Southcentral Alaska's near-term data center power planning.

MEA's board faces a 12 to 18 month decision window on converting the nonbinding term sheet to a binding PPA before the feasibility-study period expires. Watch the West Susitna Access Road EIS docket at dot.alaska.gov and the Army Corps Section 404 wetlands permit application. Those two dockets will tell you more about the coal-baseload path for JBER, Eielson, and Railbelt commercial sites than any press release out of DOE.

If Commissioner Anderson's ROD is delayed past 2028, is there a credible alternative baseload path for Southcentral Alaska's AI data center load growth, or does the region default to the natural gas supply constraints the Railbelt is already managing?
