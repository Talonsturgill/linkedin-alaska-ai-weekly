The Pentagon’s case for autonomous systems in the Arctic rests on one idea. Drones can hold a watch where people can’t. The hardware tells a colder story.

Maj. Gen. Gordon Davis, now a senior fellow at the Center for European Policy Analysis, put the frame plainly to Defense News in January. “Uncrewed systems matter in the High North not because they’re revolutionary but because they enable persistence where continuous human presence is really impractical.” He’s right about the problem. The Arctic is enormous, dark for months, and punishing on people. Manned patrol at scale isn’t realistic, and a sensor that never sleeps is worth real money. This logic underwrites a growing share of Arctic ISR investment, and capable program managers hold it for good reasons.

The frame breaks at the hardware layer. The same cold that makes human presence impractical also wrecks the machines meant to replace it. In February, the Army’s 11th Airborne Division ran cold-weather exercises and the findings were blunt. Gen. John Cogbill, who commands the division, told Breaking Defense that “everything breaks at negative 40 degrees.” Small drones that fly fine in the Lower 48 drop to ten minutes of flight time or less once temperatures fall below negative 20. Tablets crack near negative 40. The persistence argument quietly assumes a fleet that keeps working in the precise conditions where it stops working.

That gap lands in Alaska first. Arctic ISR and unmanned programs anchored at Eielson, Clear Space Force Station, and the 11th Airborne at Fort Wainwright are sized and justified on the persistence rationale. Federal contractors writing those business cases, and the program offices buying against them, are pricing autonomy as if cold is a footnote. It isn’t. A commercial airframe rated for mild weather is a different buy than one engineered for sustained operation at 40 below, and the cost, the schedule, and the vendor list all change once that requirement is written down honestly.

None of this kills the case for autonomy in the north. It sharpens it. The real question isn’t whether unmanned systems belong in the Arctic. It’s whether the cold-weather performance standard is built into the requirement before the award, or discovered afterward in the field, when a battery dies at the worst possible moment.

Should Arctic autonomy contracts carry a mandatory cold-soak standard at 40 below before the first dollar moves?
