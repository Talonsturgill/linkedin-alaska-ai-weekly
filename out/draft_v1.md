A UAF machine-learning model trained on 13 years and 700,000-plus chinook tagging records just gave Alaska's biggest fishery a tool to dodge the bycatch closures that can shut it down. Three other Alaska deadlines this week are about hosting someone else's AI economy. Only the model is an asset Alaska owns.

UAF grad student Marcel Gietzmann-Sanders, working with professors Andrew Seitz and Curry Cunningham, built the model on a U.S. Navy-funded chinook tagging program running from Southeast Alaska to the Bering Sea. It produces predictive depth charts, where chinook sit in the water column by time, temperature, and light, so pollock operators know when and where to fish to avoid them. In 2024, two boats hit enough chinook bycatch over one weekend to close the pollock fishery. Alaska pollock is the largest U.S. fishery by catch volume, and a single industry-wide closure costs the sector hundreds of millions. Operators can fold these forecasts into 2026-season trip planning now.

That's ownership. Alaska built the data, the model, and the operational edge. Compare it to three deadlines on the calendar this week, all of which ask Alaska to enable an AI economy it doesn't own.

- NSF TechAccess (LOI due June 16) would fund a state AI coordination hub at roughly $1 million a year, but only 10 hubs make Round 1. A University of Alaska-led consortium or the Alaska Workforce Innovation Board is eligible to compete or wait for Round 2 in December.
- Alaska LNG HB 381 (special session expires June 19), passed 34-5 in the House, swaps property tax for a volumetric flow tax projected near $120 to $131 million a year for Glenfarne's roughly 800-mile, $45 to $55 billion line. It's the power layer beneath any Alaska compute thesis, and it heads to a skeptical Senate.
- GVEA cost risk (bids close June 29) follows the June 5 Air Force lease solicitation at JBER, Eielson, and Clear SFS. Three of five Eielson-area sites would draw on the civilian GVEA grid, raising the prospect that co-op members, not the developer, cover the upgrade costs.

Here's the structural read. Ownership compounds. The bycatch model gets sharper every season the data grows, and the value accrues to the people who built it. Tenancy doesn't compound. You host a data center, you sell power, you stand up a hub on someone else's terms, and the leverage stays with the tenant who can leave. The model is a template, not a one-off. The question is which layer of the AI stack Alaska decides to own next.

Should Alaska treat the UAF model as the exception or as the blueprint for which layer it builds rather than rents?
