"Satellites don't work up here." That line is now shaping how DoD program officers scope Alaska's missile-defense AI spending for FY26 and FY27, and the categorical version of it is wrong.

The claim comes from a credible source. Troy Boufard, who directs UAF's Center for Arctic Security and Resilience and holds a West Point research fellowship, told MeriTalk in January that "since satellites don't work up here, we've got to figure out how to use all the other sensors, right?" The framing carries weight. FPRI echoed it in November, and CSIS analyst Kari Bingen reached for the same logic in January.

It is easy to see why. Geosynchronous satellites sit near the equatorial plane and reach only grazing elevation angles above roughly 65 to 70 degrees North. Coverage degrades and gaps open. This is real and well documented. It drove the Space Force's Arctic Satellite Broadband Mission. A planner working from a GEO baseline has good reason to push toward ground, maritime, and airborne sensors.

But the limit is GEO-specific, not categorical, and low-earth orbit refutes it directly. Per Breaking Defense (23 Feb 2026), the Army's 11th Airborne Division ran Starshield, the military Starlink network, at JPMRC Rotation 26-02 in the Yukon Training Area near Fairbanks. The only reported limitation was line of sight through camouflage netting, a concealment trade-off, not absent coverage. The link worked. And FPRI, the same defense-analyst community circulating the gap narrative, writes that SAR imaging already delivers "year-round, near-real time imagery" in the Arctic and calls an Arctic-dedicated LEO constellation "a welcome addition." That acknowledges working satellites, not a vacuum.

So the real sensor gap isn't satellite absence. It is integration latency between streams that already exist.

- LEO communications and ISR, operational today via Starshield
- SAR imaging, providing year-round near-real-time coverage now
- Over-the-horizon radar plus undersea and airborne feeds

The highest-leverage AI work is fusing those layers, not building ground sensors as if orbit were empty.

That distinction is about to get priced into contracts. Alaska-based contractors scoping Fort Greely Next Generation Interceptor sustainment and the Golden Dome intercept layer are specifying AI sensing architecture right now. A "satellites don't work" baseline biases that spend toward ground-only fusion and away from the LEO and SAR integration layer, which is exactly where Alaska's highest-value defense-tech contract exposure sits. Mis-specify the problem in FY26 and the recompetes inherit the error.

If you're scoping Arctic defense AI this cycle, are you architecting for a satellite vacuum or for the LEO and SAR feeds already overhead?
