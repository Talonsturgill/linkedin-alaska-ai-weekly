Gov. Mike Dunleavy keeps telling hyperscalers that Alaska's cold air could save a one-gigawatt data center upwards of $150 million a year. The number is real. As a reason to build here, it points the wrong way.

At Data Center World Power in Texas last October, reported by Data Center Knowledge, Dunleavy said, “Think on average, we are 30 degrees cooler than Texas, which, when you look at your PUE math, could save a one-gigawatt plant upwards of $150 million a year.” Alaska Rep. Kevin McCabe echoed the frame in an April 2026 ADN op-ed, calling the cold climate “an advantage for cooling.”

Smart people believe this for good reasons. Alaska really does average about 30 degrees Fahrenheit cooler than Texas. Free-air cooling is a proven hyperscale technique, PUE is a standard site-selection metric, and at gigawatt scale a fractional PUE gain compounds into tens of millions of dollars. Leading a pitch to a capital-allocation audience with a hard cooling number is rational.

The problem is what the number leaves out. UAF's Alaska Center for Energy and Power, in its own data-center analysis, finds cooling typically runs 5 percent or less of a hyperscale facility's ongoing operations and maintenance cost. Zeroing out cooling entirely wouldn't reach $150 million a year on most builds. Meanwhile, ACEP notes Alaska's commercial electricity rate is higher than any other U.S. state. Per 2024 EIA data cited in an ADN op-ed by Stan Jones, large commercial users on Chugach's Southcentral system pay roughly 14 to 17 cents per kWh, against about 6.12 cents in Texas, 8.99 cents in Virginia, and 6.61 cents in Washington.

That inverts the math. Here is the more accurate frame for a CFO modeling total cost of ownership at one gigawatt.

- Cooling is 5 percent or less of operating cost, so the climate edge is structurally minor.
- Power is the dominant line, and Alaska's runs 2 to 3 times competing markets.
- AI GPU-dense racks usually need liquid cooling regardless of ambient air, shrinking the free-air edge further.

As Jones put it, “Data centers don't go where electricity is merely available. They go where it is abundant, scalable and priced to support nonstop, industrial-scale demand.”

The stakes are concrete. Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, and DigitalBridge are weighing Alaska siting in the 2025 to 2026 window with the $150 million figure handed to them as the headline anchor. Due diligence will surface the rate penalty, and local developers like Stak Energy on the North Slope and Port MacKenzie promoters carry the stranded-feasibility risk when it does.

If cooling isn't the case for Alaska compute, what power-cost structure would actually make the TCO close?
