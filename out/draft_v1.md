Alaska's Legislature passed HJR 44 unanimously last month to defend ANC federal contracting rights. The machine it's defending has a single administrative valve that no state resolution can turn.

The ANC 8(a) sole-source pipeline is a four-layer federal contracting structure through which Alaska Native Corporation subsidiaries channel federal sole-source and set-aside awards, anchored in 15 U.S.C. § 637 and the ANCSA carve-out that treats ANCs as Indian tribes for contracting purposes.

The four layers, from statute to execution:

- Federal Statutory Foundation — establishes 8(a) eligibility for ANCs with no sole-source dollar ceiling and allows one ANC to hold multiple certified subsidiaries, a structural advantage unavailable to any other 8(a) participant. Controlled by Congress, backstopped by Sen. Lisa Murkowski.
- SBA Administrator Eligibility Gate — non-delegable authority under 15 U.S.C. § 637(a) to approve or deny 8(a) participation. No new applications have been approved since August 2025. Controlled by SBA Administrator Kelly Loeffler.
- ANC Subsidiary Corporate Structure — individual ANCs create separate 8(a)-certified operating subsidiaries across different NAICS codes. Chugach holds Chugach Government Solutions. Doyon holds Arctic IT, which runs Microsoft Azure services for federal agencies. Bering Straits holds the subsidiaries whose record dividends Haven Harris cited on May 14. Controlled by individual ANC boards.
- Alaska State Legislative Backstop — HJR 44 passed 40-0 in the House on April 29 and 20-0 in the Senate on May 11, coordinated by Nicole Borromeo of the ANCSA Regional Association. Raises political cost of the freeze. Does not legally bind the SBA.

The chokepoint is Layer 2, and it's asymmetric. The SBA Administrator's eligibility determination is non-delegable by statute. No court order, no state resolution, and no congressional pressure short of a statutory amendment can compel the Administrator to process applications on any timeline. In 2024, roughly 600 new 8(a) applications were approved. Since August 2025, the number is zero. That gap is an administrative decision by one Senate-confirmed officer, and every other layer of this machine is waiting on it.

The freeze isn't a legal abolition of the program. Existing certified firms continue receiving awards, and the statute is intact. But the subsidiary pipeline is narrowing through attrition. Nine-year program clocks run, subsidiaries shift NAICS codes, new projects need new entities. For Arctic IT, Koniag Government Services, Chugach Government Solutions, and their peers, this freeze lands at the worst possible moment. Federal AI spending is accelerating under the current administration's AI Action Plan, and 8(a)-certified firms hold the lowest-barrier entry point into that spending wave. The ANC-owned firms best positioned by domain expertise to compete are being prevented from standing up the new subsidiaries that would let them do it.

ANC boards should audit their existing 8(a) subsidiary certifications this week, model revenue exposure on vehicles expiring within 12 months, and accelerate bids executable under current certifications before attrition further narrows the pool. Coordinating with Sen. Murkowski's office on a targeted statutory fix or appropriations rider that imposes processing timelines on the SBA Administrator is the only lever that doesn't require the Administrator's voluntary cooperation.

If the freeze holds through late 2026, ANC-owned Alaska firms lose competitive position in the federal AI spending surge to non-ANC contractors who face no equivalent administrative barrier.

Should ANC boards pursue a statutory processing deadline as the primary lever, or is direct political pressure on the SBA Administrator the faster path given the current timeline?
