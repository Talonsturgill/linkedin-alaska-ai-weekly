Alaska’s court system built an AI chatbot to do the job of a human self-help facilitator. The bot then pointed a user to alumni of an Alaska law school that does not exist.

The chatbot is named AVA, and its design goal was stated plainly. Stacey Marz, Administrative Director of the Alaska Court System, told NBC News in January that the aim was “to basically try to replicate the services with the chatbot that we would provide with a human facilitator.” Replicate, not assist. That word is doing a lot of work, and it is the word worth examining.

The ambition is sound. Alaska runs roughly 7,000 self-represented litigants through its courts each year, across a state larger than Texas, with no law school and thin legal aid. For most rural Alaskans, the realistic alternative to a facilitator isn’t a different facilitator. It’s no help at all. A reliable chatbot answering routine procedural questions at 2am, every day, would genuinely extend access. Marz and her team aimed at the right north star.

Where it breaks is reliability, and the court has said so itself. Beyond the invented law school, Marz acknowledged in the same article that “we’re not confident that the bots can work in that fashion, because of the issues with some inaccuracies and some incompleteness.” The validated test-question set was cut from 91 down to 16. A three-month project ran more than 18 months past target. Replication of a facilitator demands near-perfect accuracy on consequential legal questions. A general-purpose model can’t guarantee that, and AVA confirmed it on the exact high-stakes question a human handles well.

There’s a second primary source pointing the same direction. A peer-reviewed Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence paper from April 2025, authored across Stanford, ANTHC, UAF, Southcentral Foundation, and Maniilaq, found that high-consequence AI deployment in Alaska needs community-engaged methodology and multi-year data integration. The authors note only 0.2 percent of more than 1,000 AI healthcare papers even mention community involvement. The field is not ready to replicate human judgment in Alaska-specific settings.

The more accurate frame isn’t “AI can’t help courts.” It’s that the design target should be augmentation on bounded, high-confidence procedural queries, not replication of open-ended judgment. The failure modes land hardest on the most legally vulnerable Alaskans. Anyone underwriting an Alaska justice, health, or social-services AI deployment on a replacement assumption should re-price the reliability gap AVA exposed before the next launch, not after. That list includes court procurement officers, govtech vendors, and federal grant writers.

Where should the line sit between a procedural question an Alaska court bot can safely answer and one that still demands a human?
