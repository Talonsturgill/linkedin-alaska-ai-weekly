NSF just named Lee Ann Munk CEO of the new Critical Mineral Accelerator Engine in Alaska, an award worth $15 million now and up to $160 million over ten years. The bet she owns is whether AI can find minerals faster than a drill rig.

Munk is a professor of geological sciences at the University of Alaska, and her home appointment sits in the UAA Department of Geological Sciences on the Anchorage campus at 3101 Science Circle. That Anchorage tie matters, because the Engine’s institutional home and its money land in Fairbanks.

On July 14, per gi.alaska.edu, NSF selected Alaska as one of 12 Regional Innovation Engines and put Munk in charge. The Engine will use artificial intelligence to help locate mineral deposits and study biological methods for extracting them, coordinating a coalition of more than 40 partners including NANA Regional Corp., Alaska Silver, and state and federal agencies. The binary she owned was whether to commit the coalition’s technology stack to AI-enabled geochemical exploration and bioextraction rather than conventional methods.

This desk reads the decision as sharp. Winning a competitively selected NSF Engine against national competition is a real strategic get, and NSF.gov confirms only 12 were funded this round. Differentiating on AI exploration and bioextraction, rather than more drilling, is where Alaska can build something the Lower 48 can’t easily copy. The fair critique isn’t the ambition, it’s concentration. A large public bet routed through a single UA-led entity carries capture risk, and AI exploration models are opaque and data-poor over Arctic terrain, so the field results need to be legible, not just promising.

In Munk’s words, “Our NSF Engine is built on the simple but ambitious idea that Alaska can lead the nation not only with the abundance of its critical mineral resources, but also in how we innovate, develop and deploy the technologies needed to produce them responsibly.”

The next call Munk owns is Year-1 deployment of the $15 million. Watch which partners get subawarded, whether the AI-exploration and bioextraction workstreams show concrete Alaska field results, and whether NSF renews toward the $160 million ceiling. The first go/no-go review inside the two-year initial window is where this bet gets tested.

On that first $15 million, should Munk prove the AI models with public Alaska field data before NSF weighs renewal toward $160 million, or does concentrating the bet in one UA-led coalition move faster than spreading it across the state?
