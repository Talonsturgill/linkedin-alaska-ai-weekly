BLM Alaska published a Federal Register Notice of Intent on May 19, 2026 to rewrite how oil and gas permits work across 23 million acres of the National Petroleum Reserve in Alaska. According to Alaska Public Media, the proposed rule would deliver permits within 60 days for qualifying projects instead of running a full NEPA review each time.

The mechanism is a federal rulemaking, led by BLM Alaska State Director Kevin Pendergast as the named responsible official, that would amend 43 CFR Part 3160 to replace project-by-project environmental review in the NPR-A with a criteria-based 60-day approval pathway for production sites, roads, pipelines, and rights-of-way.

How the layers stack:

- L1 — Industry Petition and NOI Trigger — AOGA filed the formal 43 CFR Part 3160 amendment petition on May 12, 2026; BLM Alaska responded with the Federal Register NOI a week later, opening a 45-day scoping period that closes July 6, 2026. Controlling actor: Pendergast as named responsible official.
- L2 — EIS Record of Decision Authority — Pendergast directs the full Environmental Impact Statement and will sign the Record of Decision adopting or declining the 60-day pathway. No commission vote required. Controlling actor: Pendergast, under delegated authority from the Secretary of Interior.
- L3 — 43 CFR Part 3160 Rule Amendment — The substantive codification of criteria-based approvals, subject to OIRA review at OMB, but the ROD is the BLM-controlled gate. Controlling actors: Pendergast for the ROD, OIRA for final rule clearance.
- L4 — Army Corps CWA Section 404 Jurisdiction — The BLM rule cannot eliminate Army Corps permit requirements for any stream or wetland crossings. Independent jurisdiction survives the rulemaking intact. Controlling actor: U.S. Army Corps of Engineers Alaska District Regulatory Branch.
- L5 — North Slope Borough and State Retained Authority — NSB zoning and Alaska DNR and DEC permits are not preempted; operators must clear these independent layers even on a 60-day BLM track. Controlling actors: NSB Mayor (institutional); Alaska DNR Commissioner John Crowther.

The chokepoint is L2. Pendergast's ROD is the only gate in this chain that doesn't require a multi-member vote or an external agency concurrence. He can adopt the criteria pathway or decline it on his own authority. That asymmetry is significant. A single official in Anchorage holds the binary that determines whether North Slope permitting fundamentally restructures or stays put.

Here's the desk's read: the attention is going to the ROD, but the leverage window is July 6. The EIS has minimum statutory timelines that can't be compressed below roughly 12 months from the May 2026 NOI, meaning no 60-day permits arrive before at least May 2027 under the best-case scenario. Operators who treat this as a 2026 story are mispricing their timeline. What matters right now is what gets filed in scoping, because scoping comments define which project types qualify for 60-day treatment when the rule is finally written. The 1-3 GW STAK Energy North Slope project and military data center proposals that depend on North Slope gas have real stakes in that qualifying criteria. So do Alaska Native subsistence communities, who face compressed consultation windows if the rule is adopted as AOGA petitioned it.

North Slope operators, AI infrastructure developers, and subsistence communities all have until July 6, 2026 to file scoping comments with BLM Alaska. What gets argued in that 45-day window shapes whether the final 60-day criteria pathway is narrow or broad, and who it includes. Operators who miss the scoping deadline will have substantially less influence over the qualifying criteria than those who show up now.
