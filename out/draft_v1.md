Three different Alaska officials have now told the press the same thing about data centers. They employ a crowd to build and almost no one to run. The headcount is real. The conclusion drawn from it is incomplete.

In an Anchorage Daily News op-ed in January, Roger Kaye wrote that data centers are “highly capital intensive and employ many in the construction phase, but few for operation.” Rep. Kevin McCabe echoed it in April, writing that data centers “do not employ thousands long term.” Assembly member Daniel Volland built a March guardrails op-ed on the same premise. This is cross-partisan, and it is the working assumption inside live Alaska negotiations.

It is also a fair place to start. A single campus often runs 20 to 100 direct full-time staff against hundreds of construction workers during the build. Set that operational headcount against the land, the power draw, and the tax concessions developers ask for, and skepticism is reasonable. In a rural economy where every permanent private-sector job counts, a borough assembly is right to ask what it actually gets.

Here is where the frame breaks. The facility staffing sheet is not the county employment effect. Brookings Institution research by Bahar and Wright, published in May 2026 using Bureau of Labor Statistics data across 93 counties, found that a county’s first large data center raised total private employment 4 to 5 percent over five to six years. It lifted wages 3 to 4 percent for existing workers and new hires. Hyperscale counties with four or more facilities saw a 23 percent increase in information-sector employment. Those gains run through induced local demand, supply chain, and complementary services, not the server room.

So the more accurate question is not how many badges scan into the building.

- Run lean on-site, yes, that part of the claim holds.
- But the right metric is the county employment multiplier, not the facility roster.
- And that multiplier shows up in wages for workers who never enter the campus.

This matters right now. The Anchorage Assembly guardrails ordinance and the Mat-Su Borough incentive proposals are 2026 decisions on land, power, and tax. If the “few permanent jobs” frame carries the table without the multiplier correction, Alaska either under-negotiates the community benefit it could capture or over-restricts a deal with genuine county-level upside. Allocators and assembly members should price the multiplier, not the headcount.

If the on-site number isn’t the real labor footprint, what benefit-capture terms should Alaska write into these deals to claim the multiplier rather than gift it?
