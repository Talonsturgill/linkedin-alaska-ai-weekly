Alaska's governor told a Data Center World audience a one-gigawatt facility would save $150 million a year on cooling costs versus Texas. That number deserves a closer read before bids land May 29.

Gov. Mike Dunleavy made the case at Data Center World in October 2025, later reported by Data Center Knowledge and Broadband Breakfast. His argument was direct. “Think on average, we are 30 degrees cooler than Texas, which, when you look at your PUE math, could save a one-gigawatt plant upwards of $150 million a year.” The directional logic is legitimate. Cold climates genuinely lower Power Usage Effectiveness. Iceland and Norway run data centers at PUEs near 1.1. Warm-climate facilities often hit 1.5 or worse. Free-air cooling eliminates mechanical chillers for much of the year, and at scale any PUE reduction avoids real dollars. A governor pitching his state to investors is right to lead with this.

The appeal of the claim is that PUE improvements are a known, measurable lever. Anyone who has priced a hyperscale cooling plant understands the savings are real and computable. The math feels tight.

Where it breaks is in the cooling fraction. UAF Alaska Center for Energy and Power researchers Peter Asmus, Gwen Holdmann, and Erlingur Gudleifsson published analysis in February 2024 finding that cooling costs represent 5% or less of ongoing operations and maintenance costs for data centers. Five percent. That means extracting $150 million per year in cooling savings from a one-gigawatt facility demands total annual O&M somewhere north of $3 billion, a figure that strains any reasonable cost model. The cooling math isn’t wrong in direction. It’s wrong in magnitude.

The larger cost problem sits on the electricity side. The same UAF ACEP analysis, updated in March 2025, identifies Alaska’s electricity rates as among the highest in the United States. Stan Jones reported in the Anchorage Daily News on April 12, 2026 that large commercial users on Chugach Electric’s system pay roughly 14 to 17 cents per kilowatt-hour. Virginia runs 8.99 cents. Texas runs 6.12 cents. Washington runs 6.61 cents. On a one-gigawatt load running at 80% utilization, the spread between Alaska and Texas is not a rounding error. It’s a nine-cent per kilowatt-hour structural disadvantage that compounds every hour the facility operates. Chugach spokesperson Julie Hasquet told the Anchorage Daily News on May 5, 2026 that the utility currently does not have the gas supply to serve a large data center at all.

The more accurate frame is this. Alaska’s climate advantage on cooling is real but limited in cost impact. The binding variable is electricity rate, not ambient temperature, and on that variable Alaska currently loses to every major competitor state.

This matters right now. The Air Force’s April 2026 Request for Lease Proposals at JBER, Eielson, and Clear has offers due May 29, 2026. Hyperscalers and DoD contractors evaluating those proposals may accept the $150 million cooling headline without stress-testing the electricity rate assumption underneath it. A mispriced bid built on the wrong cost driver doesn’t just lose money. It sets a precedent for how Alaska gets priced into federal compute infrastructure for the next procurement cycle.

If you were modeling a one-gigawatt facility in Alaska, what rate assumption would change the investment case from viable to unworkable?
