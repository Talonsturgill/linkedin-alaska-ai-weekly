Gov. Mike Dunleavy is selling AI data center developers a power price of five to seven cents per kilowatt hour, contracted for 20 or 30 years. The utility that would actually power those campuses says it doesn’t have the gas.

The pitch is real and it isn’t invented. Speaking to Standard & Works in January and again at Data Center World in April (reported by Broadband Breakfast), Dunleavy framed it plainly. He wants data farms and crypto operators to come to Alaska and get five-to-seven-cent gas-fired power for two or three decades.

There’s a serious case underneath it. The North Slope holds vast proven gas, stranded today only for lack of a pipeline. If the Alaska LNG Project were built, industrial users could plausibly land power below ten cents, and a contracted 20-to-30-year rate is a genuine differentiator against spot-exposed Lower 48 grids. The Alaska Gasline Development Corporation has modeled scenarios in that range. The number isn’t fiction.

It’s just not a deliverable rate today. It’s a post-pipeline projection.

The utilities themselves have been specific. Chugach Electric spokesperson Julie Hasquet told the Anchorage Daily News on May 5 that the utility currently has no gas to serve a large data center, and its Cook Inlet contract with Hilcorp expires in 2028. ADN columnist Stan Jones reported on April 12 that large commercial users on Chugach pay roughly 14 to 17 cents per kWh today, against 8.99 in Virginia, 6.12 in Texas, 6.61 in Washington. Former RCA commissioner Robert Pickett called Southcentral’s electric industry a world of hurt. The Alaska LNG Project, estimated at 44 to 60-plus billion dollars, has never reached a final investment decision after decades of trying.

The accurate frame is narrower than the pitch.

- The 5-to-7-cent rate assumes a pipeline that has no final investment decision.
- The utilities that would power a campus charge 14 to 17 cents now.
- Chugach is planning LNG imports, not new pipeline supply.
- Three 3-GW data centers would burn more than eight times the gas the entire Railbelt uses today.

This matters because capital is being calibrated against the promise. Data center developers, AI infrastructure investors, and DoD and USAF siting offices are setting commitment sizes and timelines on the five-to-seven-cent premise. If that’s a modeled post-pipeline rate and the serving utility has said it lacks the gas, those commitments carry stranded-asset risk built on a price the utility itself hasn’t offered.

Should developers underwrite a rate the utility hasn’t agreed to, or wait for a final investment decision that’s eluded Alaska for decades?
