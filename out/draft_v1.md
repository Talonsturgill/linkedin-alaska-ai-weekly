The most advanced AI now running on Alaska soil sits under a road in Utqiagvik, predicting permafrost thaw a decade out. Penn State built it. No Alaska institution owns it.

That digital twin, published June 16 in a peer-reviewed study led by Penn State civil engineering professor Ming Xiao with UAF co-author Dmitry Nicolsky, is a first-of-its-kind, physics-informed system for Arctic infrastructure. Two 1-kilometer fiber-optic cables sit buried under a road embankment, feeding a machine-learning model that forecasts subsurface ground temperature and unfrozen water content. The cables stay in the ground for roughly ten more years. Permafrost thaw could cost Arctic communities and governments billions in infrastructure damage over coming decades, so Alaska DOT, the North Slope Borough, and pipeline operators could schedule repairs before failure using a method like this. The hard problem is Alaska’s. The capability isn’t.

Same week, the same pattern. Alaska DNR’s public comment window on its preliminary best-interest finding for lease ADL 422741 closed June 15. The proposal is a 50-year lease to STAK Energy, 715.4 acres along the Dalton Highway about 26 miles south of Deadhorse, for a gas-powered facility with modular high-performance computing units. STAK is an Outside developer. June 15 was the last formal public-input gate before DNR issues a binding final decision. Alaska furnishes the ground and the gas. The compute, the IP, and the balance sheet sit elsewhere.

And the federal instrument that could change that just opened its first door. NSF’s TechAccess AI-Ready America program (NSF 26-508) puts up to $224 million across 56 hubs in three rounds, $1 million per hub per year. The Round 1 letter-of-intent deadline passed June 16, with full proposals due July 16 and later rounds running into 2027. A University of Alaska or Alaska Workforce Investment Board convener could still take a later-round hub. While that window sat open, the Legislature spent the week on the gasline tax, opening a second special session June 20 to send HB 381 to a six-member conference committee targeting July 1.

So the scorecard for one Alaska week reads like this.

- A deployed Arctic AI system, owned by a Pennsylvania university.
- A state-land compute campus, owned by an Outside developer.
- A federal capacity-building program with a still-open Alaska entry, and no public state convener moving on it yet.

The through-line is ownership. Alaska keeps supplying the ground, the gas, and the hard problems AI gets tested on, while the models and the capacity to build them stay owned somewhere else. That is a sovereignty outcome and a capital outcome, not a press release.

Who should own Alaska’s AI capability, the State, the University, the ANCs, or whoever shows up first from Outside?
