A 3 GW AI data center on Alaska’s North Slope is being called “inevitable.” The application is real and the logic is serious, but that word is carrying preconditions that didn’t exist when it was said.

The claim comes from the top. At a March 2026 DOE workshop hosted by Oak Ridge National Laboratory, STAK Energy CEO Sparrow Mahoney argued that the AI data center load shape is changing, so places like the North Slope “become not only viable, but we argue that they have become inevitable.” STAK holds an active Alaska DNR application for a one-square-mile industrial site and is recruiting capital partners for a proposed $500M, 3 GW facility.

The logic deserves a fair hearing. The Slope sits beside one of North America’s largest gas accumulations, offers land and natural cold, and skips the Railbelt grid entirely, so it doesn’t compete with urban ratepayers. Even former DNR petroleum analyst Antony Scott allowed that North Slope operators would “likely be willing to sell gas.” And modern AI inference tolerates latency better than earlier architectures, which makes remote siting near fuel more workable than it was five years ago.

The break is in the contracting. Per Anchorage Daily News reporting on the DNR filing this May, STAK had no firm gas supply agreement when it filed. The application leaves the pipeline route at “anywhere between 25 and 90 miles” and names no source field. Scott’s read was blunt. “That means they don’t have a gas supply.” Scale compounds the gap. The plant would burn more than twice the gas urban Alaska uses for all power generation and heating combined, on a Slope where producers have almost exclusively shipped oil because minimal infrastructure exists to move gas to market. The ORNL proceedings describe power from the adjacent Prudhoe Bay field, a sourcing claim the filing itself doesn’t back.

The accurate frame is conditionally possible. The site becomes buildable when three things exist that didn’t in mid-2026.

- A firm gas supply contract with a named North Slope producer
- A financed pipeline spur on a defined route
- Gas-to-market infrastructure sized to more than twice urban Alaska’s total consumption

None of that says the project is a bad idea. It says “inevitable” is the wrong word while DNR weighs the land decision and capital partners size a $500M commitment. State energy officials are already citing the claim as validation of Alaska’s AI strategy. If money or state land moves before a gas contract exists, the bet rests on an assumption nobody had demonstrated as of mid-2026, that Slope gas is commercially available to a third-party buyer at that volume.

Should capital treat the gas supply contract or the DNR lease as the real gate on this project?
