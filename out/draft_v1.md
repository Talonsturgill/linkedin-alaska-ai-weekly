Governor Dunleavy’s 2025 Computer Science Education Week proclamation says every Alaskan can already work a high-paying tech job from home. The capital backing that claim is real. The tense is wrong.

The proclamation, dated 8 December 2025 and echoed on Alaska DEED’s Computer Science program page, states that “with the advent of permanent remote work and the buildout of broadband infrastructure throughout the State, all Alaskans, urban and rural, have the ability to access high-paying, technology-driven careers from within their home communities.” It’s an aspiration grounded in real federal money, not wishful thinking.

And smart people believe it for good reason. Alaska has secured more than $1 billion in BEAD broadband funding, with 29 deployment projects underway. Remote tech work is a genuine and growing category, and some Alaskans already hold these jobs today. The premise that broadband can lift rural communities out of economic isolation is sound.

The present tense is where it breaks. Three months after the proclamation, Alaska’s own Commerce Commissioner, Julie Sande, wrote in the Juneau Empire on 24 March 2026 that the buildout is still in progress. Her figures tell the story. More than $1 billion allocated, but only “over $629 million now awarded to fifteen subgrantees.” Twenty-nine projects aiming to connect “more than 46,000 homes and small businesses.” And “around 70% of BEAD locations from Utqiagvik to Metlakatla are expected to go online” within three years. Sande still calls high-speed internet “a luxury in many Alaska communities.” FCC-standard data via BroadbandNow backs her up, showing only 68.5% of Alaskans can buy broadband meeting the 25/3 Mbps standard. Roughly three in ten can’t purchase qualifying service right now.

So the honest frame isn’t “the prerequisite is done.” It’s “we are building the prerequisite, fast, and it’s about 70% of the way there on a 2025 to 2028 timeline.”

That correction changes a real decision, and the decision is sequencing. DEED computer-science and AI-literacy grants delivered into communities that can’t yet sustain reliable broadband put the workforce cart ahead of the connectivity horse. Three actors should re-order their spend during the BEAD deployment window.

- DEED program managers writing CS-education grants against statewide assumptions.
- Rural community grant recipients building toward connectivity they don’t have yet.
- Workforce-development allocators funding remote-work training where the pipe isn’t lit.

None of this means the goal is wrong. It means the order matters, and roughly 30% of Alaska’s own BEAD target is still three years out.

So here’s the open question. Should AI-literacy and remote-work grants be sequenced behind confirmed broadband availability per community, or does training-ahead build demand that pulls the buildout faster?
