Alaska’s governor told a Data Center World audience a one-gigawatt data center would save $150 million a year on cooling costs versus Texas. That number deserves a closer read before bids land May 29.

Gov. Mike Dunleavy made the case at Data Center World in October 2025, later reported by Data Center Knowledge and Broadband Breakfast. His argument was direct. “Think on average, we are 30 degrees cooler than Texas, which, when you look at your PUE math, could save a one-gigawatt plant upwards of $150 million a year.” The directional logic is legitimate. Cold climates genuinely lower Power Usage Effectiveness. Iceland and Norway run data centers at PUEs near 1.1. Warm-climate facilities often hit 1.5 or worse. Free-air cooling eliminates mechanical chillers for much of the year, and a real PUE advantage translates to real avoided cost. A governor pitching his state to investors is right to lead with this.

Where it breaks is in the cooling fraction. UAF Alaska Center for Energy and Power researchers found in February 2024 that cooling costs represent 5% or less of ongoing data center operations and maintenance. Five percent. Getting $150 million per year out of a 5% cooling fraction implies a total O&M cost base that no operating facility of this type supports. The directional case is real. The $150 million figure doesn’t survive the cost-fraction math.

The binding cost sits on the electricity side. UAF ACEP’s March 2025 analysis found Alaska’s electricity rates are among the highest in the United States. Stan Jones reported in the Anchorage Daily News on April 12, 2026 that large commercial users on Chugach Electric’s system pay roughly 14 to 17 cents per kilowatt-hour, compared with Virginia at 8.99 cents and Texas at 6.12 cents. That’s a structural disadvantage on the cost driver that actually dominates the operating budget. Chugach Electric told the ADN on May 5, 2026 that the utility currently does not have the gas supply to serve a large data center at all.

The accurate frame is narrower. Alaska’s cold climate reduces a real but minor cost line. The deciding variable is power rate, not ambient temperature, and on that variable Alaska currently trails every major competitor state.

The Air Force’s April 2026 Request for Lease Proposals at JBER, Eielson, and Clear has offers due May 29, 2026. Hyperscalers and DoD contractors evaluating those proposals may anchor to the $150 million cooling headline without stress-testing the electricity rate underneath it. A bid priced on the wrong cost driver sets a template for how Alaska competes in federal compute procurement for the next several cycles.
