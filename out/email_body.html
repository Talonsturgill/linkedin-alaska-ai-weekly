The Air Force’s AI data center lease bids close May 29 across 4,700 acres at JBER, Eielson, and Clear SFS. Chugach Electric and Golden Valley Electric have already told the Air Force they can’t supply the gas to power them.

That’s the binding constraint nobody priced in. Under the DAF Enhanced Use Lease model, winning bidders self-fund power and water procurement, so the utilities’ position isn’t advisory. It’s a hard ceiling on what any bidder can credibly propose by the 29th. Chugach’s Hilcorp Alaska gas contract expires in 2028. Cook Inlet supply is dwindling on the curve ADN laid out May 5, with both utilities on record that they can’t serve a large data center load today. The fuel curve and the federal AI buildout curve are pointed in opposite directions, and the federal one has a deadline.

The conventional read of the JBER and Eielson push is that this is a chips-and-models story, that AK wins if it trains enough engineers and lands enough primes. That framing missed the wall. The decisive variables this week aren’t models or compute. They’re gas molecules, statutory clocks in Juneau, and a labor pool that’s contracting while the workload arrives.

The Juneau side of the ledger has two statutes running on a May 20 session-end clock. HB 217 requires a physically present, credentialed human safety operator in every commercial AV at 10,000 lb or under. The House passed it on April 30. Senate Transportation held it May 5 and holds a second hearing today. Teamsters Local 959 backs it as job protection. The Autonomous Vehicle Industry Association, Alliance for Automotive Innovation, TechNet, and R Street oppose. Whichever way it lands, it sets Alaska’s posture on autonomy procurement for years. SB 111, the right-to-repair bill, passed the Senate 15 to 5 on May 11 and gets a House Labor and Commerce hearing May 13. It forces Apple, Amazon, Google, and their peers to supply parts, tools, and documentation to independent shops at parity with authorized providers. Same May 20 deadline. That’s three statute-level clocks compressed into eight days, in a session where the AI data center bids close nine days after gavel-out.

The labor side is worse than the headlines suggest. Per Alaska Business Magazine on May 8, citing DOLWD March data, the Information sector sits at 4,100 jobs, down 4.7% year over year. Federal civilian employment sits at 13,300, down 12.5%, a loss of 1,900 positions. Net nonfarm growth held to 0.3%. The workforce that a federal AI buildout at JBER and Eielson would draw against is shrinking, not growing, in exactly the categories that matter.

One door did open. UAF signed a three-year MOU with the Defense Innovation Unit on May 8, announced by Sen. Sullivan, with initial focus on energy systems through ACEP and drone operations through ACUASI. No initial capital attached. It’s a permission slip, not a check. But it’s the only piece of institutional plumbing that got built this week, and it sits structurally adjacent to the gas problem, because ACEP is exactly the shop that should be modeling what a 2028 Hilcorp cliff means for a data center load.

The stress test is simple. Federal AI infrastructure dollars are arriving on a procurement clock. Alaska’s gas supply, its statute book, and its labor bench are all on slower clocks running the wrong direction. The May 29 bid date will reveal whether any bidder can write a credible power plan against utilities that have already said no. The May 20 session close will reveal whether the legislature ships HB 217 and SB 111 or punts. Both answers land before fiscal year end, and both shape what kind of AI economy Alaska gets to build.

If the utilities can’t supply the gas and the session ends without a clear AV and repair posture, what does a serious May 29 bid actually look like?
