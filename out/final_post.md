Anna Brawley, chair of the Anchorage Assembly, co-sponsored a June 6 rewrite that seats sworn police and the city prosecutor as voting members of the civilian body meant to review them.

Brawley chairs District 3 (West Anchorage) and took the gavel at the April 28 meeting. On June 6 she released a substantially revised AO 2026-62, the ordinance rebooting Anchorage’s civilian public safety commission, alongside co-sponsor Kameron Perez-Verdia and former member Felix Rivera.

Per the Anchorage Assembly ordinance on muni.org, the revision cut community-member commissioners from 14 to 9 and converted five agency seats (police, fire, health, emergency management, and the Department of Law) from non-voting ex-officio to full voting members. It allows up to three former public-safety employees or their family members among the community seats, and it confines the commission to closed matters and system-level policy, walling it off from personnel records, disciplinary investigations, and any non-public information. After public pushback, the Assembly voted 8-4 on June 10 to delay final action and add a hearing, according to the Anchorage Daily News.

On oversight design, this is the weaker of the two paths Brawley could have taken. Seating the operators as voters on their own review body, and barring that body from non-public information, structurally thins independent scrutiny right as APD stands up an automated-surveillance stack. The ordinance text names none of this, so be precise. It is about oversight structure, not surveillance. But this commission is the venue where APD’s Real-Time Crime Center (up to roughly 750 registered camera feeds), a pending automated license plate reader rollout, and an Axon Draft One AI report-writing trial are slated for civilian policy review. Who sits on the body and what they can see decides how real that review is.

The sympathetic read is honest too. A commission with agency buy-in can outlast a toothless one that police simply ignore. Brawley told the Anchorage Daily News on June 8, “I would not be surprised if some members of the public see this as a step back.” That is a candid line from a sponsor, not a defensive one.

The additional hearing and delayed vote land in July. Watch whether the five agency seats stay voting, whether the non-public-information bar survives amendment, and whether APD’s ALPR and camera policies actually get referred here at the Public Health and Safety Committee. As chair and co-sponsor, Brawley owns the final-vote framing.

Should the people who run APD’s cameras and ALPR hold votes on the body that reviews them, or does that seat the referee’s opponent?
