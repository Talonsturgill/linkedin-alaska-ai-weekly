On 3 August FERC opened a comment window on an amendment that would raise average annual generation at Bradley Lake, Alaska’s largest hydro plant, by about 38 percent. Standing to contest it closes 28 August.

A non-capacity amendment is how a hydro licensee changes its license without adding meaningful nameplate capacity. Five layers sit under P-8221-124.

- Licensee filing. Alaska Energy Authority filed the final amendment application on 30 June, keeping this on the amendment track, not a relicensing.
- Acceptance and standing. The Commission Secretary’s notice sets one deadline for comments, protests, and interventions. Only intervenors are parties.
- Delegated disposition. 18 CFR 375.308(b)(1) hands uncontested Part I amendment applications to the Director of FERC’s Office of Energy Projects.
- Project governance. The Bradley Lake Project Management Committee, AEA plus Chugach, Golden Valley, Homer, Matanuska, and Seward, runs the project.
- Financing framework. The AEA board advanced a financing framework on 23 July and sent it to the Railbelt utilities.

The chokepoint is layer three. The Commission has already delegated to that Director, or the Director’s designee, authority to act on uncontested amendment applications. If no protest or motion to intervene is on file at 5 p.m. Eastern on 28 August, this one is uncontested and that officer can issue the amending order, conditions and all, alone. If something is on file, the delegation doesn’t reach it. The switch is a clock, not a vote.

The nameplate arithmetic is the whole trick. The amendment would divert Dixon Glacier meltwater into Bradley Lake, raise the pool about 16 feet, and add a 0.3 MW micro-hydro turbine. About 38 percent more generation on 0.3 MW of added nameplate keeps this on the non-capacity track, exactly the class that delegation covers. So the largest identified block of new firm non-gas Railbelt energy in any regulatory queue is, procedurally, routine. That isn’t a scandal. It’s the ordinary shape of Federal Power Act administration, and it’s why the pressure point is a deadline rather than a hearing. AEA’s own page puts construction near 400 million dollars, funding source undetermined, across six years of study, permitting, and build. This is the power gate under Alaska AI, not near-term capacity for a 2028 data center.

Any Railbelt load developer, independent power producer, or large-tenant offtaker pricing against Bradley Lake energy after 2030 has this week to decide whether to intervene. Positioned already are AEA and the five purchaser utilities inside project governance. Not positioned is every non-utility developer off the docket, with no standing after that hour to contest operating conditions. The next date to hold is the AEA board’s return to final financing terms this fall.

Is party status on this amendment worth the filing cost for a non-utility load developer, or does that fight belong elsewhere?
