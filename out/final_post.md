The Department of Energy cut Alaska a check this week. About $18 million over five years, awarded to Koniag, single bidder, sole source. The structure of that check is the actual story.

Per a federal contract record reported June 5, Koniag Advisory and Business Solutions, a subsidiary of Koniag Government Services and an Alaska Native Corporation enterprise for Kodiak Archipelago shareholders, won roughly $18 million from DOE to provide programmatic support for the SBIR and STTR small-business research programs. Award ID 89303026CTT000007. The conventional read is the obvious one. The federal AI buildout is finally landing real dollars in Alaska, and an ANC enterprise captured them.

Both halves of that read are true and both are narrow. First, the award came through 8(a) sole source, the one procurement lane Alaska reliably wins. That isn’t competitive past-performance in the broad commercial base, it’s a preference program, and preference programs are politically contingent by design. Second, the contract bought program support, the grant back office, not the AI workloads themselves. Alaska captured the administration of someone else’s research money, not the research.

That matters because the other capture channels open this week are just as narrow and just as time-boxed. NSF’s TechAccess AI-Ready America program (NSF 26-508) funds one AI Coordination Hub per jurisdiction at $1 million a year for three years, and Round 1 selects only 10 hubs. The mandatory letter of intent is due June 16. The University of Alaska System is an eligible lead. Miss June 16 and Alaska defers to a December round while 10 states build first. That is a real decision in front of the state right now, not a hypothetical.

Meanwhile the rules get written Outside. The bipartisan Great American AI Act discussion draft, released June 4, proposes a three-year federal preemption of state AI laws. AFA-CWA and the AFT oppose it. LeiLauni Scheideman, president of AFA Council 30, the union for Anchorage-based Alaska Airlines flight attendants, argued against preemption in an Anchorage Daily News op-ed June 1, citing AI monitoring of cabin crews. Alaska has no enacted AI labor law, so preemption wouldn’t void a statute. It would lock the legislature out of writing one for three years.

Stack these and a pattern shows. Alaska’s share of the federal AI economy runs through a handful of contingent preference lanes and closing grant windows, while the terms are set in Washington. The Koniag award is a win. It’s also a concentration risk if 8(a) becomes the plan instead of the wedge.

If the reliable lane is the contingent one, what does Alaska build before the preference closes?
