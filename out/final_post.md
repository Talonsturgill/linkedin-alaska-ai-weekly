Federal Arctic sensor dollars are being scoped right now on a four-word premise. “Satellites don’t work up here.” Two-thirds of that premise is no longer true, and the gap is steering capex.

The line comes from Troy Boufard, who directs the Center for Arctic Security and Resilience at UAF and holds a research fellowship at West Point. Speaking to MeriTalk in January 2026, he framed the Arctic sensor problem this way. “Since satellites don’t work up here, we’ve got to figure out how to use all the other sensors, right?” It’s a credible voice saying something a lot of program offices already believe.

And for decades it was simply correct. Geostationary satellites, the GEO belt where most weather, comms, and surveillance birds sit, genuinely cannot illuminate latitudes much above 70 degrees North. Planners who learned Arctic operations before the LEO era carry that as settled fact, and they aren’t wrong about GEO. The hard part Boufard is really pointing at, fusing many sensor types into one real-time picture, is also a genuine and unsolved problem.

But the unqualified word “satellites” now quietly carries the GEO limitation onto orbits where it doesn’t apply. Iridium NEXT flies 66 satellites at 86.4-degree near-polar inclination across six planes, delivering 24/7 coverage of both poles. Its GMDSS authorization extended maritime safety coverage to Sea Area A4, the high-Arctic zone GEO physically cannot reach, for the first time. Starlink put satellites into polar orbit in 2022 and the service has been live in Alaska, the Canadian Arctic, and Scandinavia since. The real distinction isn’t satellites versus everything else. It’s GEO-belt versus LEO polar constellations.

That distinction is load-bearing because it’s writing budgets. NORAD and USNORTHCOM are scoping a 2026 to 2030 domain-awareness modernization, and Alaska-based contractors are competing on potential DAF sensor solicitations. When the premise is a blanket satellite failure, terrestrial-only pitches inherit priority and LEO-integrated architectures get underweighted before the trade study even starts.

The honest frame keeps both halves. Space-based moving-target radar really is immature, and sensor fusion really is the bottleneck. But LEO connectivity over Alaska is here today, and the question for a program office isn’t whether to skip space. It’s what the terrestrial layer should complement.

If you’re scoping an Arctic sensor portfolio, does your trade study treat LEO polar coverage as available infrastructure or write it off with the GEO belt?
