Gov. Mike Dunleavy says the roughly $44 billion Alaska LNG pipeline needs one thing to pencil. “We just need an anchor tenant or so, and that’s it.” A hyperscale AI data center is supposed to be that tenant. The pitch leaves out the harder half.

The steelman is real. A single AI data center pulling 1 to 2 gigawatts would be the first credible large in-state gas consumer Alaska has seen in a generation. Phase 1 of the in-state distribution plan has always lacked committed domestic demand. A fixed long-term anchor offtake would generate in-state revenue and shrink the financing-risk premium on a project whose core problem has never been resource. Sen. Dan Sullivan made the same case to the Legislature in January 2026, arguing that federal data centers on Alaska bases would increase domestic demand for the state’s gas and lower the cost of building and financing the pipeline.

That logic is sound in most energy markets. Demand unlocks supply, and the delegation is right that committed demand has been the chronic missing ingredient. Anyone who has watched this pipeline stall for two decades on a chicken-and-egg of no buyers should find the anchor-tenant idea genuinely attractive.

Here is where it breaks. The pitch quietly assumes the gas already exists and that it pencils. Alaska’s own utilities are on record that it doesn’t. Chugach Electric spokesperson Julie Hasquet told the Anchorage Daily News in May 2026, “We currently do not have gas to serve a large data center, but we do have generation capacity.” Golden Valley Electric confirmed the Cook Inlet gas it relied on fell from about 30 percent of its fuel supply to under 1 percent last year. The supply for a new large load is not sitting there.

The only alternative is North Slope pipeline gas, and the price works the wrong way. Wood Mackenzie, retained by AGDC, estimated Phase 1 pipeline gas at $10 to $13.70 per MMBtu against a current Cook Inlet price near $8.69. The pipeline gas a data center would buy is more expensive than the gas it would replace, not cheaper.

So the more accurate frame is this. Demand was never the only missing ingredient. Deliverable, affordable supply is the harder one, and the anchor-tenant pitch skips it. That assumption is load-bearing in AGDC’s case to private capital across the 2026 to 2028 financing windows, and in whether HB 259 data-center tax incentives rest on a supply chain that actually exists.

Should HB 259 incentives be conditioned on secured, priced gas before a single data center counts as anchor demand?
