Dunleavy vetoed SB 258 on June 18, the bill that would have barred Alaska from signing software-licensing contracts that lock the state into one vendor or restrict use to a single geography. The veto hands procurement leverage back to incumbents.

Here’s the through-line this week. The private side of Alaska’s AI buildout kept signing deals and the federal clock kept running, while the public side spent its special session saying no.

SB 258 is the cleanest case. It passed the Senate unanimously and the House 26-13, then died on the desk. Dunleavy’s verified rationale is that the restrictions “could reduce flexibility, limit access to needed products, and increase costs for agencies and local governments.” Read structurally, that’s the incumbent’s argument. The enterprise platforms state agencies run their AI tooling on get bought through exactly these single-vendor deals, and the contracts public-sector AI rides on now stay locked without a statutory backstop. Flexibility for the agency today is lock-in for the agency in 2030.

The fiscal instruments stalled the same way. On June 19 the Senate passed an amended HB 381, the LNG pipeline property-tax cut, 12-8, but its amendments (a corporate income tax on pass-throughs and a hard construction deadline) were rejected by developer Glenfarne and the governor. Glenfarne spokesperson Tim Fitzpatrick called the Senate version “unworkable, unfinanceable, and uncompetitive.” The House canceled its floor session rather than concur, killing the first special session after passing the original 34-5 on June 12. Dunleavy called a second special session for June 20 at 10 a.m. That’s the fiscal terms under the cheap-power pitch, unsettled.

Now the contrast. On June 15 Glenfarne signed a non-binding letter of intent to supply North Slope gas to Chugach Electric, the Railbelt’s anchor utility, once Phase One of the roughly 800-mile pipeline runs around 2029. That adds Chugach to a roster already including Enstar. Private momentum, while the public terms it depends on keep getting deferred.

Two more data points. GCI restored Aleutian subsea fiber on June 16 after about three weeks of satellite backup for six communities, with no root cause named, a reminder that the connectivity substrate is a single point of failure. And the Air Force’s commercial AI data-center lease proposals for three Alaska bases are due June 29.

Capital and program managers underwriting Alaska AI are pricing in a public counterparty whose software and gas terms keep getting vetoed or deferred even as private LOIs and a federal deadline advance. Can private LOIs and a federal lease clock carry the buildout while the state’s own terms stay unsettled?
