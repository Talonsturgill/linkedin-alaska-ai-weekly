Alaska's biggest federal revenue stream for rural broadband just got a new rulebook, and the Alaska Plan that's funded village connectivity for a decade expires December 31. The vetting process replacing it doesn't exist yet.

President signed the Rural Broadband Protection Act (S. 98) into law on May 11, per the White House Office of the Press Secretary. The law orders the FCC to stand up a financial and operational vetting process for every applicant and recipient of high-cost universal service broadband funds. Rulemaking is now triggered. A published FCC deadline hasn't followed yet.

The conventional read is that this is a fraud-prevention bill aimed at Lower-48 bad actors who took RDOF money and didn't build. That read is true and incomplete. The structural fact for Alaska is that the state runs on more per-capita high-cost USF dollars than anywhere else in the country, and the Alaska Plan, the 10-year fixed-formula program USAC has used to pay carriers serving high-cost Alaska, sunsets December 31. The successor Alaska Connect Fund is captured by the same new vetting framework. The funding stack didn't get cut. The eligibility math changed from formulaic to discretionary, six months before the old program dies.

Watch where capital is moving against that uncertainty. GCI Liberty reported Q1 on May 7 with revenue of $256M (down 4%), adjusted OIBDA of $93M (down 18%), and 2026 capex guidance of $290M. Three days later the board accepted Chairman John Malone's offer to redeem the Liberty Latin America 6% stake at cost, restoring roughly $107M cash and rebranding the parent to Liberty Capital Corporation. The Quintillion acquisition (up to $360M) is still pending regulatory close. Alaska's dominant carrier is concentrating its balance sheet on Alaska fiber the same week its largest subsidy line had its eligibility rules rewritten.

Compare the win-state. Koniag Technology Solutions booked a five-year $147,542,030 USDA OCIO enterprise IT contract on May 9, Small Business Set-Aside on GSA MAS, three bidders, per SAM.gov as reported. Koniag, the Kodiak ANCSA regional, did the compliance work. The federal services pipeline still pays the AK actors who navigate the architecture. Universal service is about to ask rural carriers to do the same thing on a shorter clock.

The lock-in matters because the first cohort the FCC vets will set the template for who's bankable on Alaska Connect Fund draws. Dunleavy also has until May 20 to act on HB 78, the defined-benefit pension revival the Legislature passed April 29. The state is asking carriers and contractors to bet a decade on Alaska while it can't yet fund retention for its own engineers and permit officers.

Who carries the documentation cost for small village carriers that can't staff a federal-grade compliance shop, the State, the regional ANCs, or the anchor carrier they sell transport to?
