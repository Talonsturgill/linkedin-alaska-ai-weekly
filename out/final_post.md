On June 18 FERC ordered six big organized markets, PJM, MISO, SPP, CAISO, ISO-NE, and NYISO, to fix the broken rules for connecting large AI data-center loads to the grid. Alaska’s Railbelt sits inside none of them.

That federal repair, reported by Utility Dive and Construction Dive, is a quiet admission that data-center demand has outrun the existing interconnection process. The order doesn’t name Alaska, and it can’t reach the Railbelt, because the Railbelt isn’t part of any RTO or ISO. So the structural fix FERC just compelled in two-thirds of the Lower 48 simply doesn’t apply here.

All month the Alaska data-center story has been told as a power-supply story. Cheap North Slope gas, the LNG tax fight, Cook Inlet math. Set that aside for a second. The binding constraint this week isn’t supply. It’s governance. There is no large-load interconnection rulebook in Alaska that says who connects, in what order, and on whose dime. The one body that could write it, the Regulatory Commission of Alaska, hasn’t.

The demand is already at the door. STAK Energy’s 715.4-acre, 50-year gas-powered HPC campus on the Dalton Highway just cleared its DNR comment window on June 15, with the state now reviewing toward a final best-interest finding and a build reportedly around $500 million. The Air Force has parcels at three installations open to commercial AI developers who would finance, build, and operate, with initial proposals due June 29. And on June 23 the Senate Armed Services Committee advanced the FY2027 NDAA 18-9, carrying roughly $2.6 billion in Alaska military construction per Sullivan’s office, including about $41 million for an Eielson Range Operations Center and about $50 million for launch capacity at the Kodiak spaceport. Read that last bundle as the defense-tech substrate AI rides on. That’s my read, not a claim anyone briefed.

The asymmetry is this. The capital and the load arrive on a calendar. The administrative artifact that decides who gets connected does not. When a missing rulebook is the gap, the first applicant tends to write the de facto rule by default, and everyone who comes second inherits terms they never got to negotiate.

That’s the same shape as the procurement gap this desk has flagged before. Not engineering, not even power. An artifact Alaska can author but hasn’t.

So who should write Alaska’s large-load interconnection rule before the first campus energizes and sets precedent, the RCA, the Legislature, the Railbelt utilities, or the first data-center applicant?
