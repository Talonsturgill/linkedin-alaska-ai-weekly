Governor Dunleavy is telling hyperscalers they might see “four or five cents a kilowatt hour for decades and decades to come” in Alaska. That number is the anchor under every 100 to 500 MW siting pitch the state is making through 2027. It rests on a pipeline that doesn’t exist yet.

The Governor said it to Data Center Knowledge in October and repeated it at his Data Center World keynote this April. The logic is sound on its face. Alaska sits on one of the largest untapped gas reserves on U.S. soil. If Alaska LNG reaches financial close and gets built, the capital amortizes over a very long project life, and delivered power to a single anchor industrial customer could plausibly land in the 4 to 6 cent range.

Smart allocators are listening for a real reason. Lower-48 data center developers are signing power purchase agreements at 7 to 12 cents and then waiting years in grid interconnection queues. Against that, a credible shot at nickel power for 20-plus years is worth a flight to Anchorage.

Here’s where the promise breaks. The price is real arithmetic on an unbuilt, unfinanced asset. GaffneyCline analyst Nick Fulford, briefing House Finance on May 26 via Rep. Calvin Schrage, said Alaska LNG has “a very small window of break-even profitability for a developer” under the current tax structure, with costs that may reach $57 to $60 billion under inflation. Senate Majority Leader Cathy Giessel called the developer’s construction timeline “completely unrealistic,” noting the company hadn’t even obtained financial-fitness certification from regulators, a six-month process on its own. And the utilities are blunt. Chugach Electric’s Julie Hasquet said flatly, “We currently do not have gas to serve a large data center.” Chugach’s Hilcorp supply contract runs out in 2028.

So the accurate frame is this. The 4 to 5 cent figure is a best-case delivered price from a project the state’s own legislative leadership and largest utilities call contested. It is not a power cost an operator can underwrite today. Three gaps sit between the pitch and the meter.

- No financial close, and a tax structure that barely pencils for the developer.
- No certified construction timeline, with a six-month regulatory step not yet started.
- A global gas-turbine backlog of as much as seven years before financed gas becomes power.

The stake is concrete. If a hyperscaler commits a site on the nickel-power assumption and Alaska LNG slips, the siting, the state lease and tax revenue, and the build-out vanish before ground breaks. A best-case price quoted as a planning input is a balance-sheet risk, not a procurement advantage.

Should the state pitch a best-case price on an unfinanced asset, or underwrite a bridge to firm power first?
