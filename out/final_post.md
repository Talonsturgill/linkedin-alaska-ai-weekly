This week Alaska’s most consequential AI decisions weren’t federal awards. They sat on three Alaskan desks with the clock running, and the biggest one closed on June 15.

That afternoon, Alaska DNR’s comment period shut on a preliminary best-interest finding for a 50-year lease, ADL 422741, covering 715.4 acres along the Dalton Highway about 26 miles south of Deadhorse. The tenant would be Stak Energy. The use would be a roughly $500 million modular high-performance computing campus running on on-site natural gas, with capacity that could reach 3 GW and operations targeted for 2028. Per DNR’s own notice, the comment close was the last formal window to shape the Final Finding and Decision.

Read the scale plainly. This is the largest proposed AI buildout in the state, and the deciding instrument is a routine land-lease finding. The project would need a newly built gas pipeline and could consume more natural gas than urban Alaska currently uses for power and heat combined. A decision that reshapes North Slope gas offtake for half a century is moving through the same machinery the state uses for any oil-and-gas surface lease.

Three days later, a second desk. Governor Mike Dunleavy has until June 18 to sign, veto, or let pass HB 239, an omnibus crime bill that includes a new felony for AI-generated child sexual abuse material. The Senate passed it 20-0 and the House 39-1 on May 20. If it becomes law, that single provision will be Alaska’s only AI-specific statute. The comprehensive AI governance bill SB2 and the AI task force resolution HCR3 both died, and the legislature is out until 2027. So Alaska’s entire AI rulebook now rides on one unsigned crime bill on one desk.

The same gate operates downstream. On June 9 the Wrangell Borough Assembly held a public hearing on a lease of borough land to Greensparc for an edge data center, the company’s second Alaska site after Cordova. A borough assembly, not a program office, is the approval gate in Southeast.

The power math is why these campuses bring their own generation. Per KTUU, Golden Valley Electric’s fuel-and-purchased-power rate hit a historic high of 20.7 cents per kilowatt-hour on June 1, up 61% on the quarter. No public grid in the Interior carries multi-gigawatt AI load at that price, so developers pencil their own gas instead.

Here’s the structural problem. Alaska is approving generational AI infrastructure and writing its first AI law through land-lease findings and a crime bill, with no statewide AI framework behind any of it.

Should a state commit to a 50-year, multi-gigawatt AI lease through a best-interest finding before it has any AI governance framework at all?
