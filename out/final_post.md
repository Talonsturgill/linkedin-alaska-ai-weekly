The Air Force is offering land at Eielson, Clear, and JBER for commercial AI data centers, and a Deputy Assistant Secretary called it a “unique opportunity for a true public-private partnership.” The RFP says something narrower.

The phrase comes from Robert Moriarty, Deputy Assistant Secretary of the Air Force for Installations, in Air and Space Forces Magazine on 11 April. Seven or more outlets repeated it inside a month. The framing is intuitive. The military conveys scarce, strategically valuable Arctic land at no land cost, and industry brings the capital and operations. That genuinely resembles proven federal real-property vehicles like Enhanced Use Leases. The DoD has real and growing edge-AI compute demand in the Arctic and Indo-Pacific, so reading the government as a future anchor tenant isn’t a careless leap. For a developer who can finance Arctic compute independently, free strategic land can be the whole enabling condition.

The label stops holding here. The Request for Lease Proposal, released 10 April, states that “although the Government may purchase AI Data Center services and power from the Project(s), it is under no obligation to do so” (per ClearanceJobs, 14 April, quoting the RFP). A true public-private partnership implies mutual obligation. This one commits land and nothing else. The government conveys real estate. It guarantees no revenue, no compute offtake, no demand floor. That places 100% of commercial viability risk on the lessee, who underwrites the entire build against speculative demand in a permafrost, grid, and logistics-constrained environment the RFP does not move to mitigate.

The more accurate frame is a real-estate lease program with full commercial risk transfer to the private sector. Unique site access on favorable terms, yes. A revenue partnership, no.

- The Air Force contributes land at three named installations.
- Developers contribute all capital and carry all commercial risk.
- The government has no obligation to buy capacity or power.
- Permafrost, grid, and logistics costs sit entirely on the lessee.

The business consequence lands on a specific reader. A bidder against the 29 May RFP deadline who modeled the DoD as an anchor tenant has to rebuild the pro forma and underwrite civilian and defense demand on its own. Projects priced on a phantom offtake commitment are the ones that stall after award, when revenue targets the RFP never promised fail to show. Alaska policymakers championing the deal as an economic-development anchor are leaning on a framing the RFP text contradicts.

If the government guarantees no offtake, what demand signal would actually make an Eielson or Clear data center bankable before the deadline?
