In December 2024, Alaska’s Department of Revenue announced an AI partnership that would, in its own words, change PFD processing for generations. The 2026 filing season just closed on the same paper workflow.

Deputy Commissioner Fadil Limani framed the Saige Consulting deal in big terms. He said the project “promises to revolutionize how we process PFD applications,” calling it a “true game changer for generations of Alaskans” (StateScoop, December 2024). The goal underneath that language is real and worth wanting. The PFD touches more than 630,000 eligible Alaskans, moves over a billion dollars a year, and still runs on a legacy platform full of manual steps and paper uploads. Saige projected roughly 116,000 hours saved annually. AI-assisted document handling is a credible way to get there.

So the optimism was reasonable. A specific savings figure, an obsolete system everyone agrees needs work, and a vendor hired to fix a known bottleneck. A smart reader had every reason to expect a faster, cleaner dividend season by now.

That’s the piece worth correcting. As of June 2026, the official pfd.alaska.gov site for the active filing season makes no mention of AI, Saige, or any new automated system. The division describes processing paper applications manually over the coming months, the same workflow that predated the announcement. Route Fifty reported in January 2025 that the system was expected to go live in early 2026. No public launch has been confirmed. And at the December 2024 announcement, Saige partner Charles Hu said plainly that none of the systems run automatically and an analyst still has to verify every application.

Put those three together and the accurate frame snaps into focus.

- Official source, June 2026. Same manual paper process, no AI in production.
- Vendor admission, December 2024. Human analyst review stays mandatory.
- Timeline, per Route Fifty. Early-2026 go-live, still unconfirmed 18 months on.

This was a human-in-the-loop automation pilot aimed at reducing manual burden, not eliminating it. That’s a worthy project. It just isn’t the transformation the launch quote promised, and it hasn’t shipped.

The stakes sit in state AI procurement. Vendors bidding for Alaska AI work, OIT and the Legislature weighing oversight, and hundreds of thousands of residents deciding whether to trust AI-delivered services are all sizing expectations off a flagship deployment that hasn’t produced a confirmed public system. The binding constraint here isn’t capability. It’s execution.

If you ran procurement at Revenue, what would you require before a state AI contract gets announced as transformation rather than as a pilot with milestones?
