The Air Force says its Alaska AI data center land lease “directly supports our missions and the readiness of our Airmen and Guardians.” The actual procurement document grades bidders on something else entirely.

The line comes from Robert Moriarty, Deputy Assistant Secretary of the Air Force for Installations, quoted by Alaska’s News Source on June 9. His full statement reads, “By making this land available, we are supporting the growing demands of the AI industry while generating value that directly supports our missions and the readiness of our Airmen and Guardians.” It’s a senior official making a first-person institutional claim, so it carries weight.

The claim is plausible for a real reason. Defense real estate leasing genuinely does generate funds that cycle back into base operations, and ancillary revenue does supplement readiness budgets. Co-locating commercial compute near installations could, in theory, seed dual-use infrastructure with latent defense value. If you read “supports our missions” as an indirect revenue mechanism, it survives.

Where it breaks is the Request for Lease Proposal itself. As reported on the live Alaska’s News Source page, the RLP grades offerors on one thing, how they will “minimize and mitigate impacts and risk to the surrounding community,” specifically the access to and affordability, reliability, and quality of energy, water, and communication bandwidth, plus compatibility with the local environment, culture, and infrastructure. There is no mission-integration criterion anywhere in the published evaluation standards. The outlet also notes it “has not received answers to written questions” about the program, so any readiness rationale stays in the press release, not in the instrument a bidder is actually scored against.

That gap matters because bidders optimize for what gets scored. A more accurate read of the program looks like this.

- It’s a commercial ground lease on military land, not a mission-integration contract.
- Selection turns on community impact mitigation, not defense value.
- The mission-readiness language is rationale, not a graded dimension.
- Mission-integration details aren’t in the public record at all.

So price it accordingly. The RLP deadline is June 29, 2026. A defense contractor or capital allocator that builds in mission-alignment features, or pays a premium expecting a defense-mission upside, is investing in something the evaluation does not reward. The dollars that win here are the ones spent on energy, water, and bandwidth mitigation. Everything else is a bet on a scoring criterion that isn’t written down.

If the readiness benefit is real, why isn’t it in the evaluation criteria, and would you bid differently once you read the RLP that way?
