Far North Digital is raising capital for a trans-Arctic cable on the premise that Alaska's fiber is, in co-founder Ethan Berkowitz's words, “too old, too small and too vulnerable.” The diagnosis is half right, and the half it gets wrong changes the price tag.

Berkowitz, the former Anchorage mayor now building Far North Digital, made the case in a January Anchorage Daily News op-ed. His argument is that stranded Alaska energy plus a new submarine link could anchor an AI buildout, but only if the state stops pretending its current connectivity is adequate. Read generously, he's right about the part that matters most to a hyperscaler. There is no direct trans-Pacific cable from Alaska to Tokyo or Dublin, and an Arctic route to Asian compute markets does not yet exist.

Smart people accept the broader framing because rural Alaska broadband really is thin, and the state's lone submarine link to the Lower 48 has a history of outages. When the interior and the villages are demonstrably underserved, “too old, too small and too vulnerable” sounds like plain description rather than a pitch.

Here's where it breaks. The claim sweeps in the entire existing system, and the existing system is neither old nor failing. The Alaska Center for Energy and Power at UAF says plainly on its own blog that Alaska has had fiber to the Lower 48 since 2019, and that the real missing ingredient is Asia-Pacific routing, not the home link. Governor Dunleavy said at Data Center World it's about 12 milliseconds from Alaska to the Oregon coast, which is functional low-latency connectivity, not a broken plant. And in March 2026 Alaska Communications announced a $124.5 million NTIA BEAD-funded gigabit expansion reaching Anchorage, Fairbanks, Kodiak, Homer, Kenai, Soldotna, and Delta Junction. That is active investment in the existing system, not evidence it needs replacing.

So the accurate frame is narrower and more investable. The gap is one specific thing.

- A direct trans-Pacific submarine cable to Asian markets, which does not yet exist and is genuinely capital-intensive.

Everything else is either working or being upgraded with federal money. That distinction is the whole financing question. An investor who buys the total-failure story prices a statewide rebuild. An investor who scopes the gap correctly prices a single trans-oceanic link, a far cleaner ask against the governor's 2031 readiness timeline and against Microsoft, Google, and Amazon weighing Arctic routes.

When the missing piece is one cable and not a system, should Far North Digital frame its raise as fixing Alaska's fiber or as building the one link that's actually absent?
