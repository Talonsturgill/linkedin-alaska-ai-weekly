$272 million in rural health money is moving through Alaska right now under the belief that the feds designed the program to push applicants toward AI. That belief is honest and influential. It’s also incomplete.

Rep. Genevieve Mina (D-Anchorage), who sits on Alaska’s Rural Health Transformation Program advisory council, put the received view plainly to Government Technology last month. “You name a program ‘transformation,’ but the way that the feds designed it is almost directing us to AI.”

It’s a fair read from where she sits. The CMS program bans spending on new healthcare facilities and on replacing Medicaid services, so the most common rural health investments are off the table from the start. More than 400 Alaska organizations are competing to obligate $272 million in months before it reverts to federal coffers, and AI vendors are the loudest, most organized force pitching fast deployments into that window. A legislator reviewing that proposal pool could reasonably conclude the design is an AI funnel.

The design isn’t, though, and Alaska’s own advancing proposals are the proof. The prohibition rules out buildings, not non-AI technology. Per the same GovTech reporting, the majority of proposals advancing to full application aren’t AI at all. They include the following.

- drone delivery of vaccines and pharmaceuticals to Interior villages (Tanana Chiefs Conference)
- new aircraft for rural medical supply transport (Ryan Air)
- wearable health monitors
- VR headsets for healthcare career training (Wrangell School District)
- behavioral telehealth apps

“Can’t build a clinic” is being read as “must buy an AI product.” Those aren’t the same rule.

And there’s a binding constraint the AI-mandate frame skips entirely. Connectivity. Per the Telehealth Broadband Project’s measurement data, only 6.6 percent of speed tests at 42 evaluated Alaska healthcare facilities met the 100/20 Mbps benchmark for high-quality telehealth, and more than 63 percent of Alaska consumer speed tests fell below the outdated 25/3 threshold. Cloud-dependent AI tools don’t run where the pipe doesn’t exist. For most of the communities this program targets, the grant terms aren’t the obstacle. The bandwidth is.

The more accurate frame is that RHTP channels money toward innovative service delivery in every form. Drones, aircraft, wearables, VR, and yes, AI. The timing makes the distinction urgent. Award determinations are expected this month, July 2026, and the budget period closes October 30, 2026. If the AI-first assumption wins, purchase orders go to vendor software before the connectivity and logistics spending the same program can fund and that rural delivery actually needs first.

For organizations allocating RHTP dollars this month, what should come first, the AI tool or the broadband and logistics that make it usable?
