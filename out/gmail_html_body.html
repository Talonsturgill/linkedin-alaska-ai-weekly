On June 1, GCI confirmed a subsea fiber break had cut internet and wireless to six Aleutian communities, from Unalaska to King Cove. The cable that carries the region just failed, and Alaska is being pitched to capital as the next place to host AI.

The pitch is loud right now. The Department of the Air Force offered roughly 4,700 acres across JBER, Eielson, and Clear SFS for privately financed AI data centers on leases up to 50 years, with proposals closing May 29. A roughly $500 million North Slope data-center proposal has preliminary state approval. The story sold to investors is that Alaska has land, cold air, and stranded energy, so the compute frontier moves north.

That framing prices the top of the AI stack and ignores the rail underneath it. The break this week dropped six communities, including a working Aleutian fishing hub, onto satellite backup, with full fiber repair expected within roughly one to two weeks. This is reportedly the latest of several Aleutian subsea breaks in the past year. You cannot anchor a 50-year compute thesis on a connectivity substrate that goes dark for two weeks at a time and on a grid where Chugach Electric is already on record that it has no spare gas to serve a large data center.

Now read the capital that built that substrate. According to a May ProPublica and Anchorage Daily News investigation, Alaska telecoms drew $4.6 billion in Universal Service Fund subsidies since 2016, more than $600 per Alaskan per year, the most of any state. The same investigation clocked Alaska’s broadband as the slowest in the country. GCI received $466 million two years after settling fraud allegations in the same program, and the FCC is now circulating a plan to overhaul the Alaska Plan that funds it. The subsidy backstop is under federal review at the exact moment the rail is breaking.

GCI is not standing still. It is acquiring Quintillion at a $310 million enterprise value, deepening its Arctic subsea exposure, and it carries $121.2 million in BEAD obligations to extend rural fiber. So the same balance sheet that owns the broken cable is the one capital is implicitly trusting to carry hyperscale traffic.

The data-center pitch keeps skipping one question. Who underwrites redundant connectivity on a rail that breaks repeatedly, when the subsidy program that paid for the first cable is itself under FCC review and bought the slowest broadband in the country?
